Product gifts are discussed in DMs and spreadsheets, so brands lose track of addresses, shipment status, agreed deliverables, usage rights, and whether follow-up should stop. For small direct-to-consumer ecommerce brands and lean operations teams, the useful goal is not to add another dashboard. It is to create a small, visible process that produces this outcome: **every creator sample has documented consent and terms, confirmed delivery, and a closed deliverable or no-obligation outcome**.

## Define the finish line first

Write the outcome in operational terms. A teammate should be able to look at one record and tell what happened, who owns the next action, and what is blocking completion. Use these rules as the boundary:

- Every open creator sample commitment needs one owner and a next review time
- Completion requires recorded evidence that every creator sample has documented consent and terms, confirmed delivery, and a closed deliverable or no-obligation outcome
- Automated reminders stop after verified completion or a documented closed reason
- Keep ecommerce, order, inventory, and product-information platforms as the system of record; only necessary coordination data belongs here

## A practical end-to-end workflow

### 1. Qualify the creator and record the proposed exchange

Record **Creator identity and channel** at this point, name the person responsible, and define the evidence that allows the work to move to the next step. The exit condition should be observable: either the team can confirm address, consent, and terms, or the record remains open with a reason and next action.

### 2. Confirm address, consent, and terms

Record **Campaign and product SKU** at this point, name the person responsible, and define the evidence that allows the work to move to the next step. The exit condition should be observable: either the team can reserve and ship the sample, or the record remains open with a reason and next action.

### 3. Reserve and ship the sample

Record **Relationship type and terms** at this point, name the person responsible, and define the evidence that allows the work to move to the next step. The exit condition should be observable: either the team can track delivery and contextual follow-up, or the record remains open with a reason and next action.

### 4. Track delivery and contextual follow-up

Record **Consent and shipping address** at this point, name the person responsible, and define the evidence that allows the work to move to the next step. The exit condition should be observable: either the team can record content, rights, return, or closed outcome, or the record remains open with a reason and next action.

### 5. Record content, rights, return, or closed outcome

Record **Shipment and delivery evidence** at this point, name the person responsible, and define the evidence that allows the work to move to the next step. The exit condition should be observable: either the team can close the record and review the outcome, or the record remains open with a reason and next action.

## Handle exceptions without hiding them

The process needs an explicit waiting state. Do not mark work complete because a reminder was sent or a form was opened. Keep the record open until the real exit condition is met. These events deserve a named exception path:

- a sample is unshipped near the campaign window
- delivery fails or the creator changes address
- a contracted deliverable is due or content rights are unclear

For each exception, store the reason, the next review date, and the person who can unblock it. That makes a weekly review useful instead of turning it into a search across email, chat, and spreadsheets.

## Start with one live cycle

Run the workflow for one client, location, role, order, or participant before standardizing it. At the end, remove fields nobody used, add evidence that was missing, and keep the status list short. The workflow is ready to scale when another person can operate it without asking the original owner what each row means.
