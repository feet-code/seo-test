A missed prep item is often announced verbally, but quantity, affected menu items, substitute decision, owner, and recovery time are not visible across kitchen and management. For independent restaurants and small multi-location restaurant groups, the useful goal is not to add another dashboard. It is to create a small, visible process that produces this outcome: **every service-impacting prep shortage has a quantified gap, approved response, owner, and communicated menu consequence**.

## Define the finish line first

Write the outcome in operational terms. A teammate should be able to look at one record and tell what happened, who owns the next action, and what is blocking completion. Use these rules as the boundary:

- Every open prep shortage needs one owner and a next review time
- Completion requires recorded evidence that every service-impacting prep shortage has a quantified gap, approved response, owner, and communicated menu consequence
- Automated reminders stop after verified completion or a documented closed reason
- Keep the POS, inventory, recipe, scheduling, and maintenance systems as the system of record; only necessary coordination data belongs here

## A practical end-to-end workflow

### 1. Identify the shortage against the service plan

Record **Location, shift, and station** at this point, name the person responsible, and define the evidence that allows the work to move to the next step. The exit condition should be observable: either the team can quantify available and required amount, or the record remains open with a reason and next action.

### 2. Quantify available and required amount

Record **Prep item and unit** at this point, name the person responsible, and define the evidence that allows the work to move to the next step. The exit condition should be observable: either the team can choose additional prep, substitution, purchase, or menu action, or the record remains open with a reason and next action.

### 3. Choose additional prep, substitution, purchase, or menu action

Record **Par, on-hand, and expected demand** at this point, name the person responsible, and define the evidence that allows the work to move to the next step. The exit condition should be observable: either the team can assign and execute the recovery, or the record remains open with a reason and next action.

### 4. Assign and execute the recovery

Record **Affected menu items** at this point, name the person responsible, and define the evidence that allows the work to move to the next step. The exit condition should be observable: either the team can verify supply and communicate the final status, or the record remains open with a reason and next action.

### 5. Verify supply and communicate the final status

Record **Shortage cause** at this point, name the person responsible, and define the evidence that allows the work to move to the next step. The exit condition should be observable: either the team can close the record and review the outcome, or the record remains open with a reason and next action.

## Handle exceptions without hiding them

The process needs an explicit waiting state. Do not mark work complete because a reminder was sent or a form was opened. Keep the record open until the real exit condition is met. These events deserve a named exception path:

- verified quantity falls below service demand
- the recovery action misses its ready-by time
- a substitution or outage changes guest-facing availability

For each exception, store the reason, the next review date, and the person who can unblock it. That makes a weekly review useful instead of turning it into a search across email, chat, and spreadsheets.

## Start with one live cycle

Run the workflow for one client, location, role, order, or participant before standardizing it. At the end, remove fields nobody used, add evidence that was missing, and keep the status list short. The workflow is ready to scale when another person can operate it without asking the original owner what each row means.
